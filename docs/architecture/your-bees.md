# Your bees

Part of Beeline's architecture notes; the index is [README.md](README.md).

`/bees` (`src/app/your-bees.ts` + `src/app/views/your-bees.tsx`, beeline-fiay, beeline-xegx) is a volunteer's own bees, in tabs: how far each collection year's are identified and the experts' identifications of them as they arrived, and what the bees turned out to be. It is the start of the student report's replacement, which Andony asked for on 2026-11-11: the old report was a 29-page PDF a season, of which about five pages were about the volunteer ([research/melittoflora.md](../research/melittoflora.md#the-volunteer-reports-it-hosts)). Like the front page it is a "mine" surface: it shows the effective person's bees, a second collector's included (beeline-77j), and follows the switch when somebody acts for somebody else; staff see a volunteer's by viewing Beeline as them, since `/people/:id` is admins only. Nothing links to it yet, neither the header bar nor the front page: Peter is showing it to staff first, and where it is reached from waits for a redesign of the front page (2026-11-12). It is reached by its address, and What you found by its tab.

**Bees by year** is one stacked bar per collection year (`sample_season`), split by how far each specimen's determination of record goes: to species or finer, to genus or subgenus, coarser, or not at all. It needs no dates, so it works for every atlas. Volunteers' own determinations count, as the determination of record counts them. Specimens identified to a family that is not a bee's (`src/bee-families.ts`) are left out, so a wasp caught as bycatch is never a bee "to species": 990 of production's 227,115 determinations of record on 2026-11-11. A specimen with no family yet stays in, since it may be a bee. The chart is SVG drawn on the server, with no library and no script: it prints, and its numbers are in a table beside it and in each bar's title. Its colours are the chart's own, one blue ramp with grey for "not yet", in `components.css`, validated for colour-blind readers in both modes.

**When experts identified them** reads `expert_identification` (`schema/111`), which turns a specimen's expert determinations into a story: consecutive determinations with the same name and qualifier are one identification, unless a different person Beeline knows gave it, which is an expert confirming the name (514 on the store of 2026-11-11, 469 of them confirming the collector's own). A new determination is not always news. Ecdysis's loader records the identification it calls current as a new event even when it repeats what the old Beeline held (`src/load-ecdysis.ts`), and its first night on production, 4 November 2026, recorded 36,359; the rule leaves the 14,563 that named a bee, changed a name or confirmed one. Each identification is dated by the earliest evidence of when it was made: a day the expert stated, else the moment it was entered in Ecdysis (Pacific wall-clock time, which Symbiota exports with no zone), else when an expert's sheet was sent, else when Beeline recorded one made in Beeline. A sheet's sending time is only the latest the bee could have been identified, and the page says "on or before" that day: none of the Idaho sheet's 1,832 determinations states a date. So is Beeline's own record of an Ecdysis identification that came with no entry time. A day's exact identifications and its "on or before" ones are separate entries. A name the old Beeline already held is not dated at all, though Ecdysis or a sheet later repeats it: the old Beeline knew it at a time it never recorded, so the repeat dates only the repeat, and a name with no useful date is no news (Peter, 2026-11-12). On the store of 2026-11-11 that leaves 20,902 identifications dated, where letting Ecdysis date the old Beeline's names would have dated 45,120, most of them Washington's. Ordering by when Beeline heard would have put that one night's identifications, entered between February and November 2026, all on 4 November.

The identifications are grouped by the day they were made, in Oregon's calendar, because experts work in batches across several seasons at once. Each day says how many of each collection year's bees, then what each identification did to the name an expert gave the bee before: named it for the first time, confirmed it, took it to a finer rank (89% of the store's 6,418 changes go from genus to species), corrected it, took it back to a coarser rank, or changed how sure it is. Which of those it was is read from the tree: a finer name is a descendant of the old one, a coarser one an ancestor. "Your own identification" means the name before was the person's: Washington's staff upload volunteers' identifications to Ecdysis, where they read back as experts'. The person's own names are left out of their batches, since they are not news to them, whether the determination is tied to their record or only names them; an expert's later confirmation of one is news, and says so. A confirmation is told from a repeat by the last person known to have given the name, so a repeat naming nobody in between does not hide it. An identification of something other than a bee is no bee identified, here as on the chart, and the undated count leaves it out too. Each day's names open to a list, each with the specimens it was given to linked to their pages (the first ten, and the rest behind a count that opens), so a volunteer can open the bee that changed and read its whole history.

The old Beeline's expert determinations carry no date (none of 187,743), and Ecdysis is fetched for Washington alone, so an Oregon volunteer has no batches at all: on the store of 2026-11-11, 237 volunteers had expert identifications and none dated. `/bees` counts them instead, as every bee an expert other than the volunteer named that is in no batch, read from the same stretches the batches are made of, and says they are on each specimen's page. That means reading every expert-named specimen of the person's rather than only the datable ones: 14 ms for a typical volunteer on the store of 2026-11-11, but 135 ms for the busiest account, so the front page shows its three newest days without the count. OSAC's GBIF dataset could date some of them to the year (beeline-muke).

**What you found** (`/bees/taxa`) is the second tab: every determination of record of the person's bees, rolled up through the name tree to its genus and family, families largest first. A row is a genus, with its bees, a bar against the page's biggest genus, and its species, which fold away past four, since a big collection has dozens in *Lasioglossum*; bees named only to the genus, and those with no family or no genus, are counted where they fall. A species is counted once whether or not it is "cf.", which asserts the species pending confirmation, but not for "aff." or "nr.", which say it is probably something else; the list shows every name, so a genus's fold counts names rather than species. Volunteers' own determinations count, and specimens identified as something other than a bee are left out and counted, as on the chart. On the store of 2026-11-11 the median volunteer's identified bees span 5 families, 14 genera and 18 species, the 90th percentile 34 genera and 114 species, and the largest 338 species in 67 genera.

Beside the volunteer's own, the page shows what others have found in the atlases the volunteer's samples were collected in (Peter, 2026-11-12): the summary says how many of the families, genera and species found there, by anybody, the volunteer has found, and under each genus the species others have found and the volunteer has not are folded away, muted, with how many specimens others have of each. A genus or family only others have found is a muted row of its own, "none of yours yet", after the volunteer's. Others' names are counted by the same rules as the volunteer's — bees only, no species for "aff." or "nr." — and a volunteer whose samples are in no atlas sees only their own. Reading every specimen of the atlas takes about 60 ms on production's store of 2026-11-12, whose busiest account collects across three atlases.

The tabs (`Tabs`, `src/app/views/components/tabs.tsx`) are links between pages rather than a panel switched by script: each page has its own address, works without script, and marks itself with `aria-current`. The student report's later sections — what a volunteer added, their identifications beside the experts', where to collect next — join as tabs of their own.

The query first narrows to the person's specimens with an expert determination something can date, and passes them to the view as a list of ids, which DuckDB pushes below the view's window functions: production's most prolific collector has 40,326 specimens and 746 qualify. On a copy of production's store each section takes at most 55 ms for that collector and about 30 ms for the median one.
